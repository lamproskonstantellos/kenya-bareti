Sessions

Four sessions in two days. For each one: the question, what we did and what we learned.

  • Integrated Energy Planning
  • Integration of Renewable Energy
  • Household Level
  • National Scale

How we worked

Short inputs with Kenyan numbers, then an activity. The two trainers held the introductions and both closing discussions together.

  • Kahoot quizzes

    Everyone plays on their own phone. After each question comes one sentence on the answer and its source.

  • A worked example

    On Day 1 the participants calculate the 2030 peak in pairs, with their own assumptions for growth and losses.

  • A live lab

    The Kenya Power Planning Lab is a web page with Kenya's numbers. Day 1 uses it to build a generation mix for 2035.

  • Group work in a web app

    An investment game on Day 1 and a household energy lab on Day 2. Each input ends in a decision of the group.

  • Table activities

    On Day 2 the tables answer on one shared Miro board, with one laptop per table.

  • A parking lot

    Open questions and numbers that participants challenge are noted and taken up again.

Day 1, 09:45–10:30 and 11:00–12:30. Lampros Konstantellos

Fundamentals and Methods of Integrated Energy Planning

What should Kenya build by 2035, and why?

Kenya's own data carry the examples, and every number on a slide has a source and a year. Lessons from Uruguay, Chile, South Africa, Germany and Greece give the comparison.

By the end, the participants can

  1. Describe the planning cycle and place Kenya's plans in it.
  2. Calculate the 2030 peak the way of the Least Cost Power Development Plan (LCPDP) and judge it against the scenarios.
  3. Calculate a levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) and read a screening curve at two discount rates.
  4. Formulate an investment model and test a plan for reliability and robustness.

The parts and what we did

TMP: Transmission Master Plan.

What we learned

  1. Planning is a loop, not a document.
  2. Forecasts miss, so plan with scenarios and know where each number comes from.
  3. Cheapest depends on hours, timing and the cost of capital.
  4. Least cost for one future is not robust for many: test it, and count the regret.

Day 1, 12:30–13:00 and 14:00–15:50. Tim Lorenz

Integration of Renewable Energy

How do wind and solar fit into the grid?

Once wind and solar plants are built, how does the system take their power? One web game, the Investment Simulation, runs through the session in three phases.

The participants

  1. Name the six properties that make wind and photovoltaics (PV) different from conventional power plants.
  2. Explain where and when supply and demand fall apart in Kenya, and which four levers close the gap: plant, grid, storage and demand.
  3. Explain why frequency stability has to be engineered once inverters replace spinning machines.
  4. Compare ways to pay for flexibility: the merit order, capacity payments, tariffs and contracts.

The parts and what we did

What we learned

Phase 1 of the game rewards the best site. Phase 2 shows that many plants of the same kind in the same region lower their own price. Phase 3 lets the groups add flexibility. Siting and technology are decisions about the system, not only about one project.

Day 2, 09:00–10:30 and 11:00–12:15. Tim Lorenz

Energy Modelling at Household Level

What can one home do with PV and a battery?

Can PV and a battery at home help to lower the evening peak? Four steps lead from demand to system design, simulation and optimisation. Each step ends with a group exercise in the household energy lab.

The participants

  1. Describe how households shape Kenya's evening peak, and build a demand profile from the appliances of one household.
  2. Size PV and a battery for three grid situations: grid-connected, weak grid and off-grid.
  3. Tell a simulation from an optimisation and read the results of both.
  4. Apply three levels of improvement: use less, shift loads to sunny hours, then size PV and battery.

The parts and what we did

What we learned

The lab uses one household from the first exercise to the last. The groups build the demand, guess a design, see the simulation of the guess and then meet the optimum. The difference between the guess and the optimum is the lesson of the morning.

Day 2, 12:15–13:00 and 14:00–15:45. Lampros Konstantellos

Energy Modelling at National Scale

How does the whole grid run, hour by hour?

The session follows the household session and moves the same questions to the scale of the country. The table activities ran on one Miro board.

By the end, the participants can

  1. Describe Kenya's power system in key numbers and one lesson from another country.
  2. Explain how a national model represents the system, and name the four parts of a model.
  3. Balance supply and demand over 24 hours in the lab and explain where flexibility comes from.
  4. Compare a household model with a national model: scale, objective, constraints and who decides.

The parts and what we did

What we learned

A model has four parts: decision variables, objective, constraints and parameters. Flexibility has four sources: hydro, storage, demand response and trade. The last part sets the household model next to the national model, row by row, together with Tim Lorenz.
